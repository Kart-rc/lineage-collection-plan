Days 1 to 22

The 30-day plan, day by day

Thirty calendar days is about 22 working days. Week 1 is phase 0 from the plan (decide and draw the golden graph). Weeks 2 to 4 are the first half of phase 1 (gateway edges). Open any task for how to do it and what done looks like.

Phase 1 collector

Stand up the first collector: gateway and mesh logs

Why this one first: the logs already exist, nobody has to change their code, and within an hour of running it you see which services really call which. It only sees HTTP and gRPC calls, so topics, tables and models wait for later collectors.

Make the mesh log who called whom

Envoy's default log line has no caller identity. Add the caller's mTLS identity and the x-client-id header to the JSON format, for the slice namespaces first.

istio meshConfig

    

    

      
Ship the logs into an Iceberg table

      
Most EKS clusters already send sidecar logs to CloudWatch through Fluent Bit. Add a subscription filter that forwards call lines to Firehose, which writes them to gw_access_raw. The collector only ever reads that table.

      
lineage-infra/terraform/gw_logs.tf

      
Provider field names for Firehose's Iceberg destination change between AWS provider versions, so check them against yours.

    

    

      
Write the golden graph and the alias seed

      
The golden graph is the slice's service calls as its owners know them, written down in week 1. The alias seed maps the names the logs use to one URN per service. Registry crawlers fill this table from phase 2; for now it is a hand-written file.

      
golden.json and aliases.json

    

    

      
Run it locally first

      
This script needs only Python 3. Run it as is to see the sample output below, then point it at a one-day export of your own logs. It builds the edges, checks them against the golden graph and scores the gate.

      
first_edges.py

      
python3 first_edges.py · sample output

    

    

      
Move it to EMR Serverless, hourly

      
Same logic, reading the Iceberg table and publishing evidence with the shared library. Schedule it hourly with EventBridge Scheduler or Step Functions, and add a daily rollup run so low-volume calls are not missed.

      
lineage-collectors/gateway/build_edges.py

Confirm the first edges

  
Every edge the collector finds lands in one of three buckets. The table follows the simulator, so set the clock and fixes above and read the verdict here.

  
CallStatusWhy, and what to do

  
    
    
  • Found and in the golden graph: spot-check three by hand. Open the edge's raw log rows and confirm the route and volume look right.
    • 
    
  • In the golden graph but missing: find out why before changing anything. The usual causes are a caller with no identity, a call too quiet for the hourly threshold, or a call that is not HTTP at all, which belongs to a later collector.
    • 
    
  • Found but not in the golden graph: ask the callee's owner. A real dependency nobody listed goes into the golden graph with an owner. Test or health traffic gets an exclusion rule.

Before you commit the team

  
Assumptions, risks and what must be ready

  
The 30-day plan rests on a handful of facts about your estate that this page can't check for you. This tab lists them, rates what could go wrong, and gives the checklist that decides whether day 1 can start. Your ticks here are saved in this browser only.

  

    
Riskiest assumptionEvery slice call carries a caller identity the logs can resolve.
Phase 1 counts an edge only when it knows who called. If a third of calls come from unmeshed jobs or shared service accounts, recall stalls well under 90% whatever the code does.

    
Top riskApproval to export access logs across accounts arrives late.
It blocks day 1, it sits with security and the account owners rather than your team, and in a regulated estate it is often the longest wait.

    
Blocks day 16 items
Owners, three engineers, platform AWS access, log access with security sign-off, one sample log line per source, and the repos. The checklist at the bottom tracks them.

  






  
Systems view

  
What flows in, what builds up, what flows out

  
Treat the first 30 days as a small system. The inflows are what other teams must give you; the stock is a call graph people trust; the outflows are what the business gets. Most month-one failures are a starved inflow, not a bug.

  

    
Inflows
    
      
  • Access logs from the mesh and API Gateway for the slice namespaces.
    • 
      
  • Owner knowledge: about two hours from each of six owners in week 1, then minutes a week.
    • 
      
  • Engineer time: three people for 22 working days.
    • 
      
  • Approvals: security for log export, AWS access, the mesh log-format change.
    • 
      
  • Budget: small AWS spend for slice-only logs.
    • 
    

    
Stock: the slice call graph, and trust in it

      
Trust builds when every edge traces to real log lines and owners have confirmed what they see. It drains through four leaks:

      
    
        
  • Unknown callers that never resolve to a service.
    • 
        
  • Stale edges when services change and nobody re-checks.
    • 
        
  • Noise from load tests and health checks.
    • 
        
  • Owner churn, which leaves edges nobody can confirm.
    • 
      

    
Outflows
    
      
  • A recall number against the golden graph, and a go or no-go for phase 2.
    • 
      
  • Unexpected callers, such as legacy-reporting reading payments-svc. Each one is a finding.
    • 
      
  • An unresolved-caller list sent to the owners who can fix it.
    • 
      
  • A running pipeline with alarms.
    • 
      
  • Reusable parts for phase 2: the evidence library, the topic and core v1.
    • 
    

  

  

    
R · Trust loop (reinforcing)
Accurate edges get used, use brings owner corrections, and corrections make the edges more accurate. Start it on day 30 by showing one finding nobody knew about.

    
B · Review-load loop (balancing)
More callers bring more unexpected and unresolved edges, which means more owner reviews. Review time then caps how fast you can widen. Keep exclusions and the alias table current, or this loop throttles growth.

  






  
Cost of acting and of not acting

  
What the first month costs, against what waiting costs

  

    
Acting now (30 days)
    
      
  • About three engineer-months, plus the work they would otherwise have done.
    • 
      
  • About 12 owner-hours in week 1, then a few minutes a week each.
    • 
      
  • A log-format change in the slice namespaces. It adds fields only and reverts as config.
    • 
      
  • Small AWS spend for slice-only logs. Size it from the sample hour: bytes per hour × 24 × 30.
    • 
    

    
Not acting
    
      
  • Every change like PR #482 relies on someone remembering its consumers.
    • 
      
  • Impact analysis stays a meeting or a thread for each change, and it gets slower as services grow.
    • 
      
  • Breaks surface downstream first, as in the illustrative case where a hotfix at 02:03 shows up as a DQ failure at 02:42.
    • 
      
  • Undocumented callers stay invisible until they break.
    • 
      
  • Audit and model-risk questions about where data came from are answered by hand each time.
    • 
    

    
Acting badly costs more than waiting
A graph people don't trust teaches them to ignore lineage, and that is harder to undo than having none. That is why the gate measures recall against owners' own view, and why every edge keeps a pointer to the raw lines behind it. Waiting has one cost that doesn't shrink: the approvals and owner time take as long next quarter as now. Start those requests even if the build waits.
